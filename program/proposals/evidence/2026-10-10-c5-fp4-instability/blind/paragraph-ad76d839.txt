Block-scaled low-bit number formats, in which each block of 32 or 16 values shares one scale set from the block's absolute maximum, are compared analytically and on captured tensors. A signal-to-noise analysis for blocks of Gaussian values gives closed forms for a uniform integer grid and for a floating-point grid. When the shared scale is restricted to powers of two, it overshoots the ideal scale by a factor between one and two, which costs the integer grid up to about 6 dB of signal-to-noise ratio because the unused top of its range coarsens every element, while a floating-point grid with ample dynamic range keeps a signal-to-noise ratio set by its mantissa width, independent of block size; an 8-bit floating-point scale is treated as having no overshoot. The comparison between the grids therefore turns on the block crest factor (maximum over root-mean-square), which smaller blocks reduce: the integer grid wins below a crossover crest factor of about 2.0 for power-of-two scales on blocks of 32 and about 2.4 for 8-bit floating-point scales on blocks of 16. On tensors captured from a pretrained 8-billion-parameter model the measured crest factors sit near or above these crossovers, so at 4 bits the floating-point grid wins or ties without rotation, and with the 8-bit floating-point scale on blocks of 16 the integer grid wins after an orthogonal rotation, in signal-to-noise and in direct-cast inference on twelve models; training is run only at 8 bits. A companion study of the same power-of-two block formats in language-model pretraining finds, on weight tensors, that halving the block from 32 to 16 lowers the integer grid's error about six times more than the floating-point grid's, that the best rounding rule for the power-of-two scale differs between the grids (rounding up suits the integer grid), and that its error proxy correlates with training-loss gaps; its 4-bit training runs use only floating-point grids, and only power-of-two scales.
