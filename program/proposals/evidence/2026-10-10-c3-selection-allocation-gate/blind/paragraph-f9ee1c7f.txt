Instruction-tuned language models of several sizes, general and domain-specialized, answer multiple-choice and short free-response questions, and several candidate answers are sampled per question with short outputs. For every candidate, one teacher-forced pass records the residual-stream activation at every layer at the position that produces the answer token. A standardized, L2-regularized logistic classifier of candidate correctness is fitted at a late layer, with all training and evaluation done in five-fold cross-validation grouped by question so that no question appears in both training and test folds; the operating layer is the one that maximizes the pooled within-question ranking metric. Decodability is defined as the mean over questions with both correct and incorrect candidates of the within-question ranking AUC of the classifier score, which is free of question-identity leakage by construction, whereas a probe evaluated with random splits looks accurate only through that leakage. For each question the candidate with the highest out-of-fold score is selected and its accuracy is compared with majority voting, by difficulty bin, with bootstrap intervals over questions. Across fifteen model-benchmark settings, decodability on medium-difficulty questions predicts the gain of selection over voting (correlation 0.96 in sample and 0.75 from a calibration set to deployment) with a decision boundary near an AUC of 0.60, and a stylized model derives that the boundary depends on how much voting beats a random pick of the pool, that selection gains on hard questions where sampled errors concentrate regardless of decodability, and that voting worsens with more samples there. The diagnostic is offered as an inexpensive criterion, measured on a small labelled set before deployment, for choosing hidden-state selection over majority voting.
