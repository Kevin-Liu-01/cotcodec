Given a professionally translated test corpus (sentences in English and in a second language) and released byte-level language models whose boundary predictor decides, byte by byte, where a new chunk starts, the method measures whether chunk boundaries fall at corresponding places in the two translations, and whether a mismatch would remain after the boundary rate is equalized. A frozen word aligner, run on the untokenized sentences, defines consistent cuts: positions between words where the alignment links split cleanly into a left block and a right block without crossing, paired with the matching position range in the translation. Every boundary is first snapped to a canonical character gap (whitespace runs collapsed, a boundary inside a multi-byte character moved to that character's start), so chunk-start and patch-end conventions that differ by one byte score identically. The statistic is a Dice coefficient: twice the number of cuts that carry a boundary on both sides, divided by the total number of boundaries on the two sides. Per language pair it is normalized as S = (D - D_floor) / (D_ceiling - D_floor). The floor circularly shifts one side's boundaries within each sentence, which preserves their count and spacing. The ceiling places the same per-sentence boundary budget on the aligner's own cuts, padding with random positions when the budget exceeds the number of cuts. S is computed for each model's native boundaries and for a rate-calibrated version whose per-language thresholds equalize mean chunks per sentence; the difference separates what equalizing the budget fixes from residual placement. A pre-registered three-way rule on a sentence-cluster bootstrap interval decides whether rate-calibrated boundaries already reach the budget-matched ceiling (stop) or clearly fall short of it (headroom). The verdict must agree under two aligners from different families, and a monolingual word-segmentation reference scored at its own budget tests whether any shortfall could be closed without translations. No model is trained; the output is a go or no-go for building translation-supervised boundary training.
