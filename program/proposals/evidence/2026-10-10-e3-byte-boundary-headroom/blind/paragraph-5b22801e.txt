Given a byte-level hierarchical language model whose learned router decides where chunks start, so that a large backbone runs once per chunk, the method measures and then steers where boundaries are placed. Each byte position is scored by a local hardness signal, the surprisal of the next byte under the model; boundary enrichment is the mean hardness at chunk starts divided by the mean hardness over all positions, so values above one mean that boundaries concentrate on hard-to-predict bytes. Significance is checked against a rate-matched null that circularly shifts the boundary indicator sequence, keeping the exact boundary pattern up to rotation, and is reported as a z-score. Training adds an auxiliary loss that pulls the router's boundary probability at each position toward one minus the stop-gradient probability the same model assigns to the next byte, alongside the usual compression-ratio loss, and a ladder of architecture and training changes is evaluated on bits per byte, enrichment and downstream tasks for English and German text, code and mathematics. The measurement is monolingual: it relates a sequence's boundaries to that sequence's own prediction difficulty, with no reference to a translation or to another sequence's boundaries.
