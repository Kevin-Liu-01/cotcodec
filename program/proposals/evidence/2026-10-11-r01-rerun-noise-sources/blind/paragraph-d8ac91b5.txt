Training the same neural network several times gives models whose accuracy and predictions vary from run to run, and the method separates the sources of that variation. Each source of nondeterminism, such as parameter initialization, data shuffling, data augmentation and nondeterministic GPU kernels, is controlled by its own seed or switch; to test one source, every other source is held fixed and only that one is varied across many runs, and combinations of sources are tested the same way. Variability is measured by the spread of final accuracy and by how much the trained models disagree in their predictions. Every source turns out to have nearly the same effect, about as large as all sources together. The explanation offered is instability of training as a whole: changing a single bit of one initial parameter already sends training to a very different model. Introducing nondeterminism only after a number of identical initial epochs reduces the final variability only gradually. Two remedies for the resulting run-to-run variability are proposed.
